Dear editors,

I would like to propose an essay of about 5,500 words on Germany's slump and why it has produced so little pressure for economic change.

Germany's output in 2025 was almost ten percent below its pre-2019 trend, yet in 2023 only seven percent of German workers feared for their jobs, against 38 percent in the slump of the early 2000s. The usual explanation, that the labour market has been miraculously insulated, does not survive a closer look. Measured against the output gap, unemployment has risen roughly as Germany's past recessions would predict. The striking number is elsewhere: about half of the shortfall has been written off as lost potential output, and the adjustment has run through jobs that were never created rather than through dismissals.

The essay argues that Germany is buying time again, in Wolfgang Streeck's sense, but with a new currency: demographic scarcity and a hiring freeze rather than money. Three buffers, an ageing workforce, a narrow sectoral shock and a credible welfare backstop, protect the core constituency of the export-led growth model. Young people, migrants and supplier workers carry the adjustment. Their signal reaches politics in the wrong currency: across Germany's 400 districts, the AfD's gains between 2021 and 2025 track industrial exposure, not unemployment. The reforms that do pass discipline the unprotected and leave the institutions of the insiders untouched. The 500-billion-euro infrastructure fund supplies the money for a new growth model; what it cannot supply is the coalition to carry one.

The piece draws on the growth-model literature (Baccaro and Pontusson), the dualisation literature, and my own calculations from European Commission, Federal Employment Agency and district election data, with a reproducible figure and table. It closes with two explicit tests by which the argument could fail.

I am an economist in Berlin working on German macroeconomic policy; I write in a personal capacity. A full draft is ready and I would be glad to send it.

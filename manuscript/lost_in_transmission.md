# Lost in Transmission

## How Germany’s recession-to-reform channel turned blunt

## 1. One gap, not two

In September 2023, Renate Köcher of the Allensbach Institute reported a survey reading her institute had never seen at this level in its archive. Seven percent of those in employment in Germany worried about whether their job was secure. During the comparable growth weakness between 2000 and 2005, the same item had recorded shares as high as 38 percent (Köcher 2023). The diagnostic vocabulary applied to the German economy in 2023 echoed 2005: commentators again called Germany “the sick man of Europe”, and structural decline and deindustrialisation framed the public debate. The Bundesagentur für Arbeit’s annual registered-unemployment series stood at 11.7 percent in 2005. In 2023 it was 5.7 percent. The household experience did not match the diagnosis.

The aggregate picture is unambiguous. Real GDP fell 0.9 percent in 2023 (revised down 0.6 pp in Summer 2025) and 0.5 percent in 2024 (revised down 0.3 pp), then grew 0.2 percent in 2025 (Destatis 2025e, 2026c, 2026d). Cumulative real output sits near its 2019 level, and potential output runs more than five percent below the path projected in 2019 (SVR 2024). Energy-intensive industrial production runs 17.8 percent below its 2021 level (Destatis 2026a). Consumer and residential-construction sentiment stand at series lows; business sentiment has moved sideways at levels last seen in the prolonged stagnation of 2012–13.[^1]

The household readings describe a different country. Employment averaged 46.1 million in 2024, the highest annual figure since reunification (Destatis 2025a). Registered unemployment stood at 3.021 million in March 2026, a rate of 6.4 percent – meaningfully below every recessionary peak since 1993 and about 1.84 million below the Hartz-era annual-average high of 4.86 million in 2005 (Bundesagentur für Arbeit 2026a). The Allensbach Generation Mitte study of 2024 found 38 percent of 30- to 59-year-olds reporting improved personal finances over the previous five years against 26 percent reporting deterioration (Allensbach/GDV 2024).

A first reading might attribute the gap to post-pandemic sectoral reallocation. Services expanded after 2020 and absorbed workers displaced from industry, leaving aggregate employment intact while industrial output fell. The reallocation channel is real: the net employment increase since 2019 has been concentrated in services, and post-pandemic hiring in health, education and public services is well documented. What it cannot explain is retention within the contracting sectors. Energy-intensive industries have shed roughly a fifth of their output without proportional dismissal. If reallocation were the dominant mechanism, the gap would close on the labour-shedding side – but it does not.

Two distinct gaps need separating here. The first is the gap between aggregate output and the labour market: four years of contraction and stagnation without the unemployment pass-through that every comparable episode before 2009 produced – a missing million, as section 3 will quantify. The second is the gap between elite diagnosis and household sentiment: “sick man” rhetoric against 7 percent job-security concern. The second gap is no puzzle at all – with registered unemployment at 5.7 rather than 11.7 percent, calm households are exactly what one would expect. It is the symptom. The first gap is the condition, and it is what this essay sets out to explain.

Three institutional buffers account for why the current macroeconomic malaise has not reached households at a scale comparable to earlier recessions, why the gap between macro and micro has persisted across four years rather than closing, and why the dominant political response has come through elite-initiated reform under institutional constraint rather than electoral pressure. The essay further argues that the buffers operate hierarchically, that they are eroding on three different clocks, and that the window they open for postponing structural adjustment is narrower than many current debates assume.

The argument can be placed in a familiar frame. In the growth-model literature, Germany is the paradigm case of export-led growth: an institutional ensemble – coordinated wage bargaining, works councils, patient bank finance, a welfare state organised around status maintenance – that for three decades coordinated wage restraint in the service of external competitiveness (Baccaro and Pontusson 2016; Hall and Soskice 2001). The buffers described here are not a departure from that model. They are the same ensemble running in reverse: institutions built to coordinate expansion under competitiveness constraints now coordinate an orderly retreat. What follows is, in that sense, an account of the defensive interior of the German growth model – and of what happens to a polity when its adjustment signals run through institutions designed to suppress them.

## 2. Not new, intensified

The current decoupling of aggregate output from household labour-market experience is not categorically new. It is the persistence of a buffering pattern visible since 2009 and consolidated in 2020.

One definition before the history. Reform, throughout this essay, means supply-side structural change: alterations to labour-market institutions, regulatory architecture, or the incentive structure of the welfare state. Fiscal expansion can be reform’s precondition – it can buy the investment that structural change requires – but it is not its substance. The distinction matters for reading March 2025 correctly, and for the test proposed at the end.

Pre-2009 recessions produced rapid registered-unemployment pass-through. The 1966/67 contraction cut real GDP by 0.3 percent and tripled registered unemployment from 161,000 to 459,000 in twelve months (Destatis 2025b). The 1974/75 oil shock contracted real output by 0.9 percent and pushed registered unemployment past one million for the first time since the early 1950s. The 1981/82 downturn fed into the 9.3 percent peak of 1985. The 1992/93 post-unification contraction produced the 12.7 percent peak of 1997. The pass-through across these four episodes ran on the order of 1.5 to 6 percentage points over one-to-four-year horizons, and the unemployment level never fully reverted after an episode; nothing comparable has occurred since 2009. Figure 1 shows the full pattern at a glance: through 2005, every shaded German contraction meets a rising German line; after 2009, the shading continues – 2009, 2020, 2023 – but the line no longer responds. Even the 2009 blip – three-tenths of a point against the largest postwar contraction – is the anomaly Burda and Hunt set out to explain.

A note on measurement is necessary here, because two unemployment concepts run through this essay and they are not interchangeable. The figures that carry the argument – Table 1, the 2026 rate of 6.4 percent, the missing-million counterfactual – are registered unemployment as published by the Bundesagentur für Arbeit: the administrative count, and the number German voters, media and politicians actually see. Figure 1, by contrast, draws on the Global Macro Database for cross-country comparability and splices into the ILO survey concept for recent decades, which runs some 2 to 2.5 percentage points below the registered rate. The figure illustrates the pattern; the registered series carries the evidence. Wherever the two diverge for recent years, the official BA figures are authoritative.[^2]

Whether the labour-market signal then translated into reform varied considerably. The 1966/67 episode produced the Stability and Growth Act within a year. The 1992/93 contraction generated the Hartz reforms only a decade later, after then-chancellor Schröder’s coalition assembled the political conditions; the labour-market signal alone did not deliver reform on a predictable horizon. The historical correlation between recession and reform is therefore not a tight one; it conditions on coalition arithmetic and partisan competition. The current break is a weakening of an already imperfect transmission, not the failure of a deterministic mechanism.

2009 was the first clear instance of significant buffering. Real GDP fell 5.7 percent – the largest postwar single-year contraction. Registered unemployment rose only to 8.1 percent. Kurzarbeit (short-time work) peaked at 1.44 million in May 2009. At the time, observers read the pattern as exceptional: short-time work as an emergency response to a deep but short shock. Burda and Hunt (2011) document that working-time accounts (Arbeitszeitkonten), not short-time work, were the dominant intensive-margin adjustment mechanism. Short-time work played a secondary, complementary role, with private working-time reductions absorbing most of the labour-input adjustment. 2020 reproduced the buffering pattern in starker form: pandemic real GDP fell 4.1 percent, short-time work reached six million in April, and registered unemployment rose only to 5.9 percent. Both episodes were treated as crisis-specific.

And in both episodes, buffering was vindicated. The 2009 hoarding preserved firm-specific human capital and matching relationships through a shock that proved transitory; when world demand returned in 2010–11, German industry restarted at full strength while competitors rebuilt their workforces. The “labour market miracle” was, in retrospect, optimal option-value preservation. This is precisely what makes the current episode analytically treacherous: the same behaviour, the same institutions, the same calm households – but a different shock. Whether buffering is wisdom or pathology depends entirely on whether the shock is transitory, and section 4 takes that question up.

2022–2026 differs from these episodes in duration. The shock has lasted four years, and the buffering apparatus has held throughout. What was emergency in 2009 and pandemic-specific in 2020 has become the baseline mode of macro-to-household transmission.

The pattern can be standardised. Dividing the unemployment response by the cumulative output shortfall against pre-crisis trend growth yields an episode-specific transmission coefficient – percentage points of registered unemployment per point of foregone output. Across the pre-2009 episodes this coefficient ran between roughly 0.2 and 0.35; since 2009 it has run between zero and 0.15, with 2009 itself – the largest shock – at approximately zero (Figure 2). A cross-country check sharpens what is German about this. The 2022 energy shock hit the whole euro area, yet euro-area unemployment has since fallen to an all-time low of 6.1 percent (Eurostat 2026) – insulation is partly a European phenomenon, built on short-time-work architectures that several countries adapted from the German template after 2009, and reinforced everywhere by ageing workforces. Within that pattern, Germany is the extreme case on both margins. It combines the deepest cumulative output stagnation among the large euro-area economies with an unemployment response that, while unique in the bloc – Germany is the only major member whose harmonised ILO rate has risen materially since 2022, from about 3 to 4 percent, while Italy’s fell from 8 to around 5, a divergence visible in Figure 1, where the Italian and French lines fall through the very years the German line stops responding to anything at all – remains small against the output damage. A pre-2009 Germany would have converted this output record into an unemployment shock on the order of twice what has been observed, as the counterfactual below indicates. The buffer endowment, not the shock, is what distinguishes the German trajectory.

## 3. Three buffers, ranked

Three mechanisms produce the decoupling, and they operate in a hierarchy that matters for diagnosis. Demography determines what firms can rationally do with weak demand. Sectoral concentration keeps the shock narrow enough for that calculation to hold. The welfare state stands behind both – a backstop whose call, so far, has largely not come.

A back-of-envelope counterfactual sizes what the buffers are jointly absorbing. Across advanced economies, Okun coefficients cluster between roughly −0.25 and −0.55, averaging about −0.4 (Ball, Leigh and Loungani 2017); Germany’s annual Okun relationship was loose even before 2009 (Burda and Hunt 2011), but what Table 1 documents is multi-year ratcheting rather than a tight annual law – each pre-2009 contraction lifted registered unemployment by 1.5 to 6 percentage points over one-to-four-year horizons, and the level did not fully revert. Applying the cross-country benchmark of −0.4 to a cumulative shortfall against the pre-crisis potential path of more than five percent (SVR 2024) implies registered unemployment some 2 percentage points higher than observed – roughly 4 million rather than 3 million; the historical German pass-through pattern from Table 1 points to a similar or larger figure. The assumptions are deliberately coarse: a stable elasticity, a shortfall measured against projected potential, no feedback from unemployment to output. The point is the order of magnitude: the buffers are absorbing a missing million of registered unemployed. How that absorption distributes cannot be identified from aggregates alone, but the flow evidence below assigns the overwhelming share to demographically rational hoarding under conditions of sectoral concentration, with welfare absorption playing a smaller role than the public debate around Bürgergeld suggests.

### Demographic scarcity is the dominant mechanism

Firms decide retention at the level of replacement risk, and demographic conditions tilt that decision towards hoarding.

Destatis projects that 13.4 million members of the labour force will reach statutory retirement age over the next 15 years. That implies an average of roughly 850,000 to 950,000 gross retirement-age entries per year, drawn from the wide cohorts born 1955–1969 (Destatis 2025c). The relevant variable for hiring decisions is gross retirement-age entry, not net change in the active population: a firm that lets a trained 55-year-old go cannot expect to replace them from the cohort behind, even if migration keeps the headline labour force stable. Net active-population change is a separate calculation. Recent net migration and medium-term projection assumptions fall in the broad range of a few hundred thousand per year; Fuchs, Söhnlein and Weber (2021) find that only an annual net immigration of about 400,000 persons would keep the labour-force potential approximately constant over the long run. Stability of the headline conceals tightening at the experienced-worker margin, where retention pressure is highest.

The firm-level evidence is direct, not merely inferred from aggregates. The Beveridge data show the signature of hoarding: registered vacancies have nearly halved, from almost 2 million in late 2022 to 1.03 million in the third quarter of 2025, while the ratio of unemployed persons per vacancy more than doubled from 1.2 to 2.8 (IAB 2025) – firms have stopped searching without proportionally starting to separate. The ifo Institute’s employment surveys describe the same behaviour from the inside: firms leave positions unfilled rather than dismiss staff (ifo 2025a). The Kurzarbeit data complete the picture of a shift from public subsidy towards private calculation: the monthly average ran around 300,000 to 320,000 in 2024, depending on data vintage, against 1.44 million in May 2009 and 5.99 million in April 2020 (Bundesagentur für Arbeit 2025a; IAB 2022). Firms now retain workers without state support – workers that pre-2009 elasticities would have seen dismissed.

One objection deserves a direct answer: demography appears on both sides of the ledger. The same labour-force contraction that makes hoarding rational also depresses potential growth and is thus part of the shock being buffered. This is true, and it conditions rather than undermines the argument. As a shock, demography operates slowly and on the supply side; energy prices and external demand, not labour scarcity, drove the 2022–26 contraction. As a buffer, demography operates immediately and on firm-level retention decisions. The two roles run on different clocks – which is precisely why the buffer can hold now and fail later, when the supply-side effect catches up with the retention calculus (section 5).

### Sectoral concentration is the enabling condition

The shock is narrow, which is why hoarding copes.

Energy-intensive industries such as chemicals, metals, paper, glass and parts of automotive supply ran 17.8 percent below 2021 output through the first eleven months of 2025; in February 2026, production rose 1.9 percent month-on-month and was unchanged year-on-year (calendar-adjusted), but the level remained materially below the 2021 average, with the cumulative shortfall still in the high-teens percent on the most recent calendar-adjusted index (Destatis 2026a; Destatis 2026b). The remainder of manufacturing is roughly flat and services are expanding. Industrial electricity in Germany ran at 20.3 cents per kilowatt-hour in the H2 2022 / H1 2023 comparison, against 8.4 cents in the United States and China (Prognos/vbw 2023); SVR 2025 documents Chinese export prices in August 2025 standing 17.3 percent below their 2022 average while euro-area export prices have risen 14.4 percent (SVR 2025, Kurzfassung Ziff. 4 / ch. 1 Ziff. 14).

Chemical giant BASF’s Ludwigshafen complex makes the picture concrete. The site employs roughly 39,000 people across 10 km² – the largest integrated chemical complex in the world and the dominant employer in the Rhein-Neckar conurbation. On 24 February 2023, BASF announced (P-23-131) the closure of one of its two Ludwigshafen ammonia plants together with downstream nitrogen-fertiliser, caprolactam, TDI and adipic-acid capacities; the closures were estimated to affect approximately 700 jobs in Ludwigshafen, embedded in a separate non-production cost-savings programme with net effects on around 2,600 positions globally (BASF 2023). Decisions to rebuild integrated capacities locally or to source intermediates from Antwerp and the Gulf are ten- to thirty-year choices that no fiscal envelope reverses on a five-year horizon. The political geography of the contraction is the geography of energy-intensive employment: acute in Ludwigshafen, Salzgitter, Zwickau, Bitterfeld; far away from Munich, Hamburg, Berlin.

Holding sectoral breadth constant at the 1992–93 pattern – when contraction was broad – even scarce labour would not have remained on payrolls. The narrowness of the current shock is doing work demography cannot do alone.

### Welfare-state absorption is the backstop in waiting

The third buffer is the one most discussed in public and, so far, the least used. The flow evidence is instructive precisely because it shows absorption that has not materialised at scale.

Roughly 5.47 million people received Bürgergeld (the basic income support that replaced Arbeitslosengeld II in 2023) on average across January–June 2025 – down about 1.6 percent year-on-year, not up. Of the 3.82 million erwerbsfähige Leistungsberechtigte (employable benefit recipients) in March 2026, only 1.10 million were registered as unemployed under SGB II; the remainder were in top-up arrangements alongside paid work, in caring roles, in training, or carrying medical placement barriers (Bundesagentur für Arbeit 2025a; Bundesagentur für Arbeit 2026b; Bertelsmann Stiftung 2025). Broader SGB II payment claims amounted to about 46.9 billion euros in 2024, including housing, social-insurance and integration components – a large standing commitment, but not a surge tracking the industrial contraction. The operative absorption channels to date are narrower: public-sector employment, which reached 5.4 million in mid-2024, up 95,900 year-on-year (Destatis 2025d) and now roughly 11.7 percent of all employed persons – the highest share since the privatisations of rail and postal services in the 1990s – and the contributory unemployment-insurance system, whose strain shows in the Federal Employment Agency’s (BA) accounts. The BA posted a 5.23-billion-euro deficit in 2025 and projects 3.97 billion for 2026, with cumulative debt to the federal government reaching up to 6.22 billion euros by end-2026 (Bundesagentur für Arbeit 2025b); part of that deficit is itself the cost of the hoarding apparatus – Kurzarbeit and ALG I – rather than of transfer absorption proper. Meanwhile the producing sector excluding construction has shed roughly half a million positions – about one percent of total employment – since 2019, to 7.9 million in 2025 (Destatis 2026f), losses that public hiring and the insurance system have so far covered without visible strain on households.

The correct reading is therefore not that welfare is currently absorbing the missing million, but that it is positioned to absorb whatever hoarding releases – and that this positioning is itself load-bearing. Firms can run scheduled, socially-compatible reduction paths because the system behind them is credible; workers can experience industrial decline as early retirement or negotiated transition rather than as unemployment. The backstop shapes behaviour before it pays out. Whether it has the fiscal capacity to actually pay out, when the call comes, is the question of section 5.

The East German precedent shows what the backstop can and cannot do: net transfers since 1990 running into the low trillions have sustained incomes, yet eastern GDP per capita remains at roughly 78 percent of western levels with convergence stalled for half a decade (Röhl 2025), and the share of eastern Germans describing themselves as second-class citizens has persisted near two-fifths across three decades (42 percent in Allensbach’s 2009 reading, 43 percent in Infratest dimap’s 2023 survey), with AfD strength in Thuringia and Saxony consolidating against this backdrop. Material support sustains income; it does not by itself reproduce the productive base or political identification with it.

### Who the buffers protect – and who they do not

The buffers are not distributionally neutral, and the asymmetry matters for the politics. Hoarding protects insiders: tenured, skilled, covered by collective agreements, disproportionately older. The adjustment that does occur runs through the margins first – expiring fixed-term contracts, agency workers, hiring freezes that close doors on labour-market entrants. The Porsche sequence is exemplary: before the announced reduction of permanent positions, roughly 2,000 fixed-term production contracts simply lapsed (Porsche 2025). The employment guarantees at Volkswagen and Audi cover core workforces; they do not cover the supplier chains, where the insolvencies cluster. And the regional incidence compounds the contractual one: the unprotected margin concentrates precisely in the energy-intensive geography – Zwickau, Bitterfeld, the supplier belts – that the salience analysis below identifies as AfD terrain; ifo president Clemens Fuest has warned in the same vein that industrial shrinkage threatens Germany’s rural regions in particular, with consequences he calls explicitly political as well as economic (ifo 2026b). The transposition of economic distress into cultural politics, noted below as a competing reading, has a mechanism: the buffer system delivers calm to the protected majority and routes the residual adjustment onto groups and regions with the weakest attachment to the political mainstream. Insulation in the aggregate, exposure at the margin.

### Interaction

Hoarding, sectoral concentration and the welfare backstop compose a chain rather than three independent layers. Demographic scarcity makes hoarding rational; without it, firms revert to dismissals at pre-2009 elasticities. Sectoral concentration keeps the contraction narrow enough that hoarding suffices and the backstop is rarely called. The backstop, in turn, makes scheduled wind-downs negotiable instead of contested – it converts potential dismissal shocks into managed flows. A broad shock would break the chain at the first link. The 1966/67 recession was less severe at the macro level than 2022–26, yet it was politically more consequential, because none of these layers existed. What has changed is the institutional layer between the labour market and the household.

The configuration invites a comparison that should be drawn precisely in order to be limited. Japan after 1992 also combined demographic contraction, employment maintenance, low unemployment amid stagnation, and reform by elite initiative rather than electoral mandate. But Japan buffered a broad, economy-wide balance-sheet and demand shock, absorbed it through firm-internal redeployment within a lifetime-employment norm, and did so in a unitary state without a significant populist-right party. Germany is buffering a narrow, sectoral supply shock, absorbing it through an institutional apparatus external to the firm, inside a federal system dense with veto points and with a blocking-minority right already seated. The Japanese lesson is that buffered stagnation can persist for decades without producing either rupture or reform; the German divergence is that the political system is far less likely to remain quiescent while it does.

## 4. Why the gap persists

A wide gap between aggregate weakness and household experience would be self-correcting if it produced adjustment. It blocks adjustment on both the economic and the political side, though through mechanisms that are gradated rather than absolute.

### The economic channel: hoarding and uncertainty suppress investment

Firms hoard labour and refuse to invest. Both behaviours are rational responses to high uncertainty, not market failure.

The option-value logic is standard (Dixit and Pindyck 1994). When investment is irreversible and demand, input prices and regulation are uncertain, waiting carries positive value. Firms commit only when expected returns clearly exceed the value of the option to wait. German firms confront three uncertainties simultaneously: a Chinese export-price decline of 17.3 percent against a euro-area rise of 14.4 percent (SVR 2025); industrial electricity at 2.4 times US and Chinese levels (Prognos/vbw 2023); and continued flux in climate and industrial policy in Berlin and Brussels.

Here the vindication of 2009 must be confronted directly, because it is the strongest objection to everything that follows. If buffering preserved option value through a transitory shock then, why is identical behaviour pathological now? The answer is that the option-value defence of buffering has a validity condition: the shock must be plausibly transitory, so that the preserved match capital has a use to return to. Every element of the current configuration argues that this condition has lapsed. The energy-price differential is a level shift anchored in geology and geopolitics, not a cyclical spike; the Chinese competition in chemicals, machinery and automotive is capacity built, not demand deferred; the SVR has diagnosed structural rather than cyclical weakness in three successive Annual Reports (SVR 2024, Kurzfassung §2); and the firms themselves reveal their beliefs in their location decisions – BASF is not mothballing Ludwigshafen capacity, it is closing it while expanding in Zhanjiang. Buffering against a transitory shock preserves options; buffering against a permanent one merely defers the exercise of an option that is expiring. The behaviour is individually rational either way – which is precisely why no decentralised correction occurs – but its social return has flipped sign. That is the difference between 2009 and now, and it is why four years matter: duration is the evidence on which the transitory hypothesis has failed.

The data show what deferred exercise looks like in the aggregate. Real (price-adjusted) equipment investment fell 5.4 percent in 2024 (Destatis 2026c post-Sommerüberarbeitung; first-release figure −5.5 percent in Destatis 2025f), followed by another weak year in 2025 – making the 2024–25 investment contraction persistent rather than one-off. Capacity utilisation in manufacturing fell to 76.5 percent in October 2024 and stood at 77.5 percent in January 2026, against a 1991-onward mean of 83.2 percent for manufacturing specifically (ifo 2026a). With idle capacity that wide – and with ECB Bank Lending Survey results showing renewed net tightening of credit standards on loans to euro-area firms in Q4 2025, Germany among the countries where banks anticipated continued tightening into Q1 2026 – neither demand pull nor credit supply is forcing the investment decision (ECB 2026a; ECB 2026b); firms with retained workforces and stable services demand are choosing not to add capital. The labour-hoarding decision and the investment-deferral decision reinforce each other – a firm that carries its workforce through the downturn faces a low marginal return on new equipment as long as existing capital sits idle, and the option value of waiting dominates on both margins.

Households read the same aggregates and substitute saving for spending. Real wages rose 3.1 percent in 2024 (Destatis), the strongest gain since the series began in 2008, and a further 1.9 percent in 2025, still slightly below the pre-crisis level (Destatis 2026e). Real private consumption grew only 0.5 percent over the same year despite the income gains (Destatis 2026c post-Sommerüberarbeitung; first release +0.3 percent), and the household saving rate stood at 11.2 percent in 2024. The wedge – real income up, consumption near flat – is the channel: deposit accumulation rather than spending. The GfK/NIM willingness-to-save indicator reached 14.0 in January 2024 (last seen August 2008), then climbed to 18.7 in December 2025 – at the time the highest since June 2008 – and to 18.9 in February 2026, a fresh post-2008 high. By the April 2026 release, willingness-to-save had eased to 16.1, but the headline Consumer Climate fell to −33.3 points for May 2026, the weakest reading since February 2023, on income-expectation losses linked to the Iran conflict and energy-price pressures (NIM 2024; NIM 2025; NIM 2026a; NIM 2026b). Domestic demand, which would otherwise substitute for weakening exports, is not contributing.

### The political channel: weakened transmission, not closed channels

Macro weakness used to reach the political system through three channels: electoral incentive, fiscal capacity, and coalition competition. All three now operate at reduced strength, through three distinct mechanisms – salience, institutional veto points, fragmentation.

Salience. The median voter does not experience the current stagnation as personal crisis. With 46.1 million employed and only 7 percent of workers reporting job-security concern in 2023, the electoral return on reflation is low. The February 2025 federal election produced a CDU/CSU result of 28.5 percent in first place and an AfD result of 20.8 percent in second place – roughly doubling its 10.3 percent share from 2021 (Bundeswahlleiterin 2025). A defensible competing reading is that the AfD share is itself the pressure signal – economic distress transposed into cultural politics rather than reflationary demand. The distributional analysis of section 3 supplies the mechanism for that transposition: the buffer system delivers calm to the protected majority and concentrates residual adjustment on the unprotected margin, contractually and regionally. But the transposition is what insulation looks like from inside the median voter’s experience: stable-employment voters do not produce the kind of reflationary mandate that layoff-exposed voters produced in 1982 or 2003.

Institutional veto points. Article 109 of the Basic Law sets the general balanced-budget principle; Article 115 operationalises the federal rule, including the 0.35 percent structural-borrowing limit; Article 143d contains transitional provisions from the original debt-brake reform. The Bundesverfassungsgericht’s ruling 2 BvF 1/22 of 15 November 2023 voided the Ampel coalition’s reallocation of 60 billion euros to the Climate and Transformation Fund (BVerfG 2023). The federal structure adds further veto points at Bundesrat level. Constraints can be loosened, as in March 2025, but only at high coordination cost.

Fragmentation. The Ampel collapsed in November 2024 over budget conflict. The February 2025 election produced a Bundestag in which AfD and Die Linke together hold a blocking minority on constitutional amendments. On 18 March 2025 the 20th Bundestag adopted the constitutional-amendment bill (Drucksache 20/15096) by 512 votes to 206, with no abstentions, exceeding the 489-vote two-thirds threshold; the Bundesrat cleared the package on 21 March, and CDU and SPD timed both votes for the outgoing chamber, where they still commanded two-thirds (Deutscher Bundestag 2025a; Bundesrat 2025). The 2025 constitutional amendment modified Articles 109 and 115 and inserted a new Article 143h. It exempts from the structural debt rule expenditure on defence, civil and population protection, intelligence services, IT security and military aid to states under unlawful armed attack – to the extent the relevant outlays exceed 1 percent of nominal GDP – and authorises a special fund (Sondervermögen Infrastruktur und Klimaneutralität, SVIK) of up to €500 billion.

The sequence is consistent with the buffer-and-channel argument rather than against it – and note that under the definition above, March 2025 is fiscal expansion, not reform. Passage required a constitutional ruling, a coalition collapse, a blocking-minority election outcome, and elite coordination to vote before the new chamber convened. The package reached the floor under the cover of an exiting majority. It created the precondition for structural change; it did not enact any.

The SVR states the diagnosis more cautiously but no less significantly: the decoupling of German industry from the world economy suggests that German weakness is not only cyclical but also has structural causes (SVR 2024, Kurzfassung §2). The advisory body has now made this argument in three successive Annual Reports without it producing the policy response that diagnostic consensus would historically have triggered. The clearest non-decision is administrative simplification. BDI, DIHK, DGB, SVR and successive Annual Reports of the Nationaler Normenkontrollrat have called for substantive reduction of reporting obligations and approval procedures since 2022, with no organised expert counter-coalition. The Bürokratieentlastungsgesetz IV of 2024 delivered selected relief measures but fell short of the broader simplification agenda the Normenkontrollrat urged; the Scholz government’s Meseberg programme did not reach implementation; the Merz government promised substantial bureaucracy reduction and so far delivers individual measures rather than structural reform. The delay carries little organised political cost: the beneficiaries are dispersed small and medium-sized firms, while the losers of genuine simplification are administrative units inside ministries and Länder bureaucracies.

## 5. Three clocks

The buffers are eroding on visible timelines.

The demographic clock has the longest horizon and operates mechanically. Retirement pressure intensifies through the late 2020s and is steepest in the early 2030s, with the 1964 peak cohort of 1.36 million births reaching 67 in 2031. Hoarding works only while firms can hoard against partially replaceable cohorts; once retirement-age entries exceed retention capacity, production contracts to match available labour, or wages rise sharply enough to reallocate workers – most plausibly some combination, varying by sector. The transition window is roughly 2028 to 2034. Note what failure of this buffer looks like: when labour supply contracts, registered unemployment tends to fall, not rise. The demographic buffer does not fail into the unemployment statistics; it fails into output and income contraction. The implication reaches beyond this episode – the classic transmission chain, recession to unemployment to electoral pressure, may not merely be dampened but permanently disabled, with macroeconomic pain arriving henceforth as fiscal strain and income stagnation rather than as job loss. That is why the failure sequence sketched in section 6 runs fiscal-first.

The fiscal clock is shorter and has produced its first signals. The BA’s 2025 deficit of 5.23 billion euros is the first sustained deficit after years of reserves. Bürgergeld, public-sector employment, and the demographically driven pension, care and health envelopes compound mechanically; the Bundesbank’s most recent projection has the general-government deficit ratio rising from 2.4 percent in 2025 to about 4 percent in 2026 and 4.5 percent in 2027, with BA loan accumulation and rising pension and care claims pressing on the federal budget over the same horizon (Bundesbank 2026a). The 500-billion-euro SVIK envelope expands fiscal space for investment, not for welfare. Dullien, Gerards Iglesias, Hüther and Rietzler’s joint IMK–IW estimate puts the public investment gap at just under 600 billion euros over ten years – larger than SVIK, meaning the existing fiscal expansion is closer to a down payment than to a programme. The backstop of section 3 is thus being squeezed before its call arrives: the moment hoarding expires and welfare absorption is actually needed at scale coincides with the moment pension, care and defence claims peak.

The capability clock is the shortest and most consequential, and it operates in tipping points rather than gradients. Once an automotive supplier cluster loses its critical mass of engineers, a chemical site is decommissioned, or a regional toolmaking ecosystem falls below viability, reconstruction costs multiples of preservation. Energy-intensive output at about 82 percent of its 2021 level reflects ten- to thirty-year location decisions, and discrete tipping events are likely within five to ten years – recognisable as such only in retrospect.

The evidence of erosion is already on the record. Volkswagen, IG Metall and the works council reached the Zukunft Volkswagen agreement on 20 December 2024, providing for socially-compatible reductions of more than 35,000 positions at Volkswagen AG’s German locations by 2030, with employment guarantees extended to end-2030 (Volkswagen 2024). Porsche has announced reductions of about 3,900 positions including agency staff by 2029 (Porsche 2025). Audi’s Zukunftsvereinbarung of 17 March 2025 sets out the elimination of up to 7,500 positions in Germany by 2029, exclusively in indirect (non-production) areas, alongside an employment guarantee extended to end-2033 (Audi 2025). Across the VW group, the 2025 annual report indicates a target of about 50,000 fewer positions in Germany by 2030. Creditreform recorded 23,900 corporate insolvencies in 2025, an 8.3 percent rise on 2024 (22,070) and the highest count in over a decade, with damage to creditors estimated at €57 billion and roughly 285,000 employees affected (Creditreform 2025). Falkensteg’s transformation-consulting series counted 471 large insolvencies (turnover ≥ €10 million) in 2025, a 25 percent year-on-year rise; automotive suppliers contributed 59 cases (unchanged from 2024) and remained one of the worst-affected segments – Falkensteg projects a further rise to about 62 supplier insolvencies in 2026 (Falkensteg 2026). These figures do not contradict the hoarding mechanism; they show it eroding in real time. Insolvency-driven separations bypass the retention decision entirely – a firm that exits does not hoard. And the negotiated reduction paths at Volkswagen, Audi and Porsche, with employment guarantees to 2030 and 2033, are hoarding with an expiry date: the buffer formalised, scheduled, and terminal.

The clocks converge unevenly. Demographic pressure is steepest through the late 2020s and early 2030s; fiscal tightening is already under way at the BA and will compound within the parliament now seated; capability erosion is continuous, with discrete losses scattered across the next decade.

## 6. What follows

The buffers are buying time. They are not adjustment.

The diagnosis points to a narrower agenda than current Berlin debates assume. The fiscal ceiling has been the dominant question since the 2023 ruling; the March 2025 package and the SVIK envelope have settled it for a decade. The binding constraints now lie elsewhere, and they translate into operational instruments rather than diagnostic categories.

First, administrative capacity. A fiscal envelope reaches the economy through procurement, planning, environmental review and Länder execution. The operational instrument is a federal-Länder staffing compact – federal co-funding of permitting and procurement personnel at Länder and Kommune level, conditional on agreed throughput targets (cases cleared per quarter, average decision time, share of contested decisions overturned). Without it, additional fiscal space converts to spending only on long lags.

Second, permitting and infrastructure timelines. Median permitting times for grid expansion, rail and large industrial projects are well-documented constraints. The instrument is statutory hard-stop deadlines (a generalised Genehmigungsfiktion regime) for grid, rail and large industrial approvals at all three federal levels, with default approval triggered by missed deadlines absent specified objection grounds. The acceleration statutes (Beschleunigungsgesetze) of recent years show what is feasible. Whether SVIK is built out in the 2027–2032 window or in the 2034–2040 window depends on this.

Third, labour reallocation architecture. The transition from energy-intensive manufacturing employment to whatever follows it is the central reallocation problem of the next decade – and, given the distributional asymmetry of section 3, the place where the buffer system’s unprotected margin either gets an exit route or hardens into permanent political alienation. Current instruments – Kurzarbeit, Bürgergeld, Transfergesellschaften – are designed for cyclical absorption, not multi-year sectoral transition. The instruments worth specifying are individual training accounts portable across employers (extending the Bildungsgutschein into a multi-year entitlement), regional reallocation premia targeting specific cluster transitions (Saarland steel, Baden-Württemberg automotive supply, Sachsen-Anhalt chemicals), and a clearer division of labour between the BA and Länder economic ministries on industrial transition cases.

The risk if execution does not arrive is straightforward. The buffers fail unevenly, not all at once, and the first failure determines the political setting for the others. The most plausible sequence is fiscal-first – and the demographic logic of section 5 explains why: because buffer failure does not register as unemployment, it registers in the budget. A BA loan trajectory running against pension, care and defence claims simultaneously would force a reduction in welfare-state generosity at the moment the demographic squeeze is steepest – the backstop cut just as its call arrives. A capability-first failure – a major automotive supplier collapse cascading through a regional cluster – would arrive faster but on smaller fiscal scale. Either reaches a parliament with a blocking minority on the right already in place. Reform under those conditions is harder than under the conditions of March 2025, which relied on a configuration – outgoing two-thirds majority, incoming blocking minority not yet seated – that was historically specific.

The transmission from macro weakness to political pressure that drove most major German reforms since 1967 is operating at reduced amplitude, and may in its classic unemployment-borne form be gone for good. Reform will come either through elite initiative while the buffers last, or through rupture when the first one fails.

The thesis is falsifiable, and the test should be stated so that the thesis can actually lose. Registered unemployment staying below seven percent is not the test – low unemployment is consistent both with self-correction and with a demographically disabled signal channel, so it discriminates between nothing. The discriminating evidence is twofold. If real equipment investment returns to sustained positive growth over several years without prior structural reform, the option-value diagnosis of section 4 is wrong: uncertainty resolved itself and the buffers were never blocking adjustment. And if a structural reform – under the supply-side definition of section 2 – is announced in the election programme of the largest governing party and enacted without constitutional amendment within the first half of a legislative term, the political-channel argument is wrong: ordinary electoral politics delivered what this essay claims only elite initiative or rupture can. Either outcome, observed over the next two parliamentary cycles, falsifies the buffer-and-channel argument. Their absence, against continued buffer erosion on the three clocks, corroborates it.

Which returns the essay to Köcher’s seven percent. The calmest job-security reading in the history of German survey research is not the opposite of the crisis. It is what this crisis looks like: a missing million absorbed so completely that the people it would have comprised do not know who they are. Buffered stagnation produces no victims who can identify themselves, no constituency for adjustment, no signal in the channel that once carried one. The number that should reassure is the diagnosis.
